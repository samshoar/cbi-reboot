Machine-to-machine payments in the AiFi era

Chad Harper, Kevin Leffew, Lincoln Murr, and Sam Shoar · October 7, 2026

The internet has a second customer. Its payment layer will decide who can participate.

Executive summaryExplore x402Payment economicsPolicy implicationsFull paper

Executive summary

Every decade, a new way of using the internet becomes dominant, and each shift changes who participates in the digital economy. The 2010s shift from desktop to mobile brought billions of new people online. The 2020s shift from mobile to agents is different. It does more than change the device. It introduces a second customer: software that retrieves, compares, and increasingly transacts on a human’s behalf at a scale and speed the web has never had to serve.

The web’s old bargain has broken down. For years, publishers and developers allowed crawlers (automated programs that download, index, and reuse pages at scale) because search engines sent readers back to them. Generative AI changes that exchange: systems crawl to answer rather than to refer, while automated traffic now makes up a large and growing share of web activity. Google search referrals to publishers fell by roughly a third globally from November 2024 to November 2025, widening the gap between the value extracted from content and data and the revenue returned to those who produce it. A functioning per-use market compensates every publisher that sets a price, not only those large enough to negotiate bilateral deals.

A payment layer for the second customer is now emerging. Most sites today face a binary choice: allow automated traffic for free or block it before it reaches the site. Cloudflare and AWS are both moving toward a third option, in which agents pay for access rather than being turned away. x402, an open standard built on the HTTP 402 “Payment Required” response code, provides the mechanism. A server responds to an unpaid request with a price; the agent pays, retries the request, and receives the resource. What matters about x402 is not that it selects one settlement asset or one provider. It is that it standardizes the payment handshake itself, leaving room for competing settlement schemes rather than routing agentic commerce through the same two card networks that intermediate most online payments today.

This paper makes three claims. First, legacy payment rails cannot economically support the high-frequency, low-value core of agentic commerce. Fixed per-transaction costs, underwriting barriers, reversal risk, and identity models built for people rather than machines make many machine-to-machine transactions uneconomic. For the smallest transactions, the cost gap is three to four orders of magnitude. Second, stablecoins are today the strongest fit for this market because they combine low-cost settlement, rapid confirmation, programmability, and global reach. Third, whether this layer develops on open, interoperable standards or inside proprietary marketplaces and closed billing systems will determine whether agentic commerce repeats the fragmentation and weak competition of the systems it replaces.

The policy stakes are global. A long tail of publishers, developers, data providers, and agents will need to settle transactions across borders and around the clock. The amounts will often be too small, and the commercial relationships too fleeting, for legacy systems. Policymakers should therefore focus less on fitting this market into existing categories and more on preserving the conditions it needs to operate: micropayment economics, interoperable identity, open protocols, per-use payments that compensate creators, and room for multiple compliant settlement schemes.

What should policy preserve?

Micropayment economics

Preserve viable per-use transactions rather than importing fixed costs and relationship requirements from higher-value commerce.

Identity, authorization, and wallet controls

Build compliance around the principal, the agent’s mandate, and bounded spending authority. A low network fee does not remove legal obligations.

Open protocols and competition

Keep payment handshakes interoperable and discovery accessible across providers. Open protocols should allow multiple compliant settlement schemes.

Creator compensation

Per-use payments can compensate publishers and developers that authorize access, including those too small for bilateral licensing deals.

International coordination

Coordinate early so cross-border standards do not default to incompatible proprietary billing systems.

The trade-off is not just price

Cards remain useful for larger purchases and offer dispute protections. Onchain confirmation removes card-style chargebacks, but does not guarantee resource delivery, redemption, or legal finality outside the network. Initial confirmation and stronger finality can have different timing.
